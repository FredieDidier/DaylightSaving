## 1. Introdução, desenho (340 → 321 palavras)

**Antes**

> This paper uses Brazil's abolition of DST to estimate how many additional road deaths occur once travel patterns have adjusted to darker evenings, and whether brighter mornings offset those deaths. Abolition turned one hour of every summer evening from daylight to darkness. In São Paulo in mid-January, for example, the sun set at 7:58 p.m. on the clock under DST and now sets at 6:58 p.m., so the hour from 6:58 to 7:58 p.m. was the last hour of sunlight under DST and has been the first hour after sunset since abolition. We call it the hour that turned dark. It is the hour after sunset in standard time, the clock without DST, so it falls at a different clock time in each municipality and on each date. We compute that hour from the solar position at each municipal seat on each date, and do the same for the hour after standard-time sunrise, which went the other way, from darkness to daylight. We use a triple-difference design that compares the 11 states observing DST in every southern-hemisphere summer from 2008/09 to 2018/19 with 12 states that did not. The estimate contrasts changes before and after abolition in the gap between the summer window and April–August, focusing on the hour that turned dark. Because the affected hour follows local sunset across municipalities and dates, a confounding regional trend would need to align with this shifting hour to explain the estimate. The hours immediately before and after experienced smaller changes in **ambient light, from full sun to low sun or from twilight to night,** and therefore provide a further check on whether the response follows the change in light. A second design uses every clock hour of the day, each weighted by how much darker or lighter abolition made **it, computed from the position of the sun.** With a separate effect for each clock hour on each date, it tests whether the deaths follow the sun or the clock, and it counts the net change over the day without selecting particular hours.

## 2. Introdução, tendências (189 → 152 palavras)

**Antes**

> For road deaths, the season-by-season estimates before abolition show no differential trend. Without the pandemic months the estimate is 23 log points; in the summer window alone, without the off-season comparison, it is 34 and stable across the five seasons without DST. An imputation estimator that extrapolates the pre-abolition trend yields a similar point estimate, although with substantially less precision. **The result is also robust to excluding any single state or season.** On federal highways, injury crashes show a trend before abolition, and both federal highway estimates shrink once a trend is allowed. We therefore take the magnitude of the effect from road **deaths. We** use federal highway records to verify the change in light and to investigate the **underlying** mechanisms. **For crashes per vehicle, we focus on seasons without a pre-trend; for pedestrian deaths relative to other road users, we extrapolate the pre-abolition trend.** The rise is concentrated in the hour that turned dark and the next one, which went from twilight to night. Road deaths at midday and at night, whose light did not change and where an effect of sleep would also appear, do not rise.

## 3. Introdução, noite contra manhã (231 → 196 palavras)

**Antes**

> Road deaths in the hour after sunrise, which turned light, show no detectable change (−3 log points, standard error 12). In the hour that turned dark, road deaths exceeded the counterfactual by 36 per summer window, an estimate that requires no assumption about the other hours of the day. A count for the whole day requires an assumption about those hours. When every hour of the day is weighted by how much darker or lighter it became, and hours that remained in full daylight or full darkness are assumed unaffected, abolition added 62 road deaths per summer window, from November 15 to February 14 (standard error 12; permutation p = 0.001). The count includes only deaths at the crash scene with a recorded time. Estimated over the whole day without assuming that hours whose light did not change were unaffected, the change is too imprecise to confirm the count or rule it out. A decline in deaths in the hours whose light did not change, large enough to cancel the count, could come from trips moving out of those hours, but the traffic counts show no such shift. They cannot exclude a smaller decline (Section 5.8). **In the model that weights each hour by its change in light, a given change in darkness has a larger effect on road deaths in the evening than in the morning (permutation p = 0.001).**

## 4. Introdução, trocas de relógio (239 → 199 palavras)

**Antes**

> The clock changes before 2019 show the same change of light before travel had time to adjust: for three weeks after each change, the evening hour was dark (after February) or light (after October) while travel kept to the same clock hours. Read as the effect of a darker evening, the changes give 42 log points (standard error 14) and abolition 29. The 29 log points are an average over the whole summer window, **however, and the effect differs across its weeks.** Abolition raised road deaths in the hour that turned dark by 58 log points (13) from mid-November to early December and by 49 (16) in the second half of January, but left no detectable change from Christmas to **mid-January (December 24 to January 15).** The weeks after the clock changes fall while schools are in session, as do the weeks from mid-November to early **December, and in** those weeks the effect of abolition is statistically indistinguishable from the effect at the clock **changes. ## 5. Seção 5.9 (621 → 573 palavras)

Vira três parágrafos: as trocas de relógio, as partes do verão e a comparação na mesma época do ano letivo. A frase das rodovias federais sobe para junto dos números que ela acompanha (no fim, parecia falar das semanas de aula).

**Antes**

> The clock changes before 2019 produced the same change of light as abolition, before travel had time to adjust. When the clocks went back each February, the evening hour 0 went from daylight to dark overnight, while work, school, and travel kept to the same clock hours; when they went forward each spring, the reverse. Online Appendix Table B9 compares hour 0 in the three weeks after each change with the three weeks before, in the treated relative to the control states, and uses the same calendar days of the seasons after abolition, when no clock moved, to net out the seasonal change. In the three weeks after the clocks went back, road deaths in hour 0 rose by 54.6 log points (21.9); after they went forward, deaths fell by 29.9 (18.5). Read as the effect of a darker evening, the two average 42.3 (13.8), compared with 29.2 after abolition. Each change also adds or removes an hour of sleep, which acts on every hour of the day and pushes both transition estimates toward zero; compared with hours of the same day, which nets it out, the transitions give 79.1 (14.2) and abolition 27.7 (12.4). In one model that allows for the overlap of the two samples, abolition minus the clock changes is −13.1 (12.5) in the first comparison and −51.4 (18.3) in the second. Only the within-day comparison, which absorbs day-level shocks common to all hours, including those associated with sleep, yields a statistically significant difference between the transition and abolition estimates. The two settings also differ in the time of year, and the effect of abolition is not the same across the summer window (Online Appendix Table B2). From November 15 to December 7, while schools are in session, abolition raised road deaths in hour 0 by 58.1 log points (12.6). From December 24 to January 15 it is −12.3 (16.8). From January 16 to 31 it is 49.1 (15.6), although schools are still on break then in every treated state but Goiás (São Paulo, 2018; Conselho Estadual de Educação de Goiás, 2018; Agência Minas, 2023). The school calendar alone therefore cannot explain how the estimate varies across the summer window: no increase is detectable from Christmas to mid-January, while the late-January school break shows about as large an effect as the school weeks. The three weeks after each clock change fall while schools are in session, the same part of the school year as the weeks from mid-November to early December. Compared with abolition in those weeks, the clock changes are not distinguishable from it: the difference is 15.8 (18.0) in hour 0 and −22.2 (20.9) in the comparison with hours of the same day (Online Appendix Table B9). The gap over the whole window thus points mainly to the weeks from Christmas to mid-January rather than to a uniformly smaller effect after travel adjusted. In the school weeks, the effect of a darker evening after five summers is close to its effect in the weeks after a clock change, though the intervals leave room for a moderate decline. Before abolition the parts of the window moved alike (Online Appendix Table B2). Federal highway traffic shows no detectable difference between the weeks from Christmas to mid-January and the school weeks, whether counted in hour 0, for trucks and buses alone, or over the whole day (Online Appendix Table B3). These checks do not rule out holiday travel on other roads as an explanation, and the data do not say why no increase is detectable from Christmas to mid-January. On federal highways the same comparison implies 121 log points for deaths at the clock changes and 54 after abolition, and about the same for injury crashes (20.6 and 22.2).

## 6. Conclusão, primeiro parágrafo (152 → 129 palavras)

**Antes**

> When Brazil stopped moving its clocks, the evening hour that lost its sunlight became deadlier on the road, disproportionately for pedestrians, and the morning hour that gained light did not become detectably safer. When only deaths at the crash scene with a recorded time are counted, and hours that remained in full daylight or full darkness are assumed unaffected, abolition of DST added 62 road deaths from mid-November to mid-February each year, and about 74 if the effect is extended to the rest of the season. In the **weeks when schools were in session, the effect** of **abolition over the five summers without DST** was **close to** the effect at the clock changes, when **the light moved overnight and** travel had no time to **adjust; across the summer, the increase was not detectable from Christmas to mid-January but was detectable in late January, when most treated states were still on school break.**

**Depois**

## 7. Conclusão, último parágrafo (188 → 162 palavras)

"Can" vira "may": a Seção 5.6 diz que a tabela da iluminação não estabelece o efeito.

**Antes**

> For Brazil, the estimates speak to the choice between reinstating seasonal DST and maintaining year-round standard time. At the reference value of a statistical life, the crash-scene deaths with a recorded time cost an estimated 114 million 2025 international dollars per summer window. The estimates are less directly transferable to other settings. In summer, seasonal and permanent DST set the same clock, so the estimates are the cost, in summer evenings, of choosing permanent standard time over either. They say nothing about winter, when permanent DST would darken the mornings, and they are least informative at latitudes higher than Brazil's. Nor do they weigh the clock changes, energy use, or the effects of the clock on sleep. Part of what people did to protect themselves, driving less in the hour that turned dark, is already in the estimates. **Within these limits, the evening hour that lost its light became deadlier on the road, deaths in the morning hour that gained it did not detectably decline, and** a disproportionate share of the **lives lost are pedestrians, whom** street lighting and road design **can** protect when the clock does not.
